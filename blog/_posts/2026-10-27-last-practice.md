Allen Holub [recently posted](https://bsky.app/profile/allenholub.bsky.social/post/3myamvgu5ei2e) something interesting about agile software development:

> Though I'm probably nuts to flog the Agile dead horse,
> I have to point out that the _only_ concrete practice in the Agile Manifesto is the retrospective.
> […] If Agile is a "methodology" (it isn't),
> the only method it requires is continuous improvement.

Indeed, not only does the [Manifesto for Agile Software Development](https://agilemanifesto.org) only mention one practice, but after its four values, it is the _last_ of the twelve principles:

> At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

I found this interesting, because while the agile manifesto focused on values and principles, our concrete _practices_ reveal how we interpret agile software development.

## Revealed preferences

Scrum eventually became unfashionable, among software development teams, and Kanban proved too difficult to become standard practice.
Since then, it has become hard for software development teams to identify the least controversial way to work.

In economics, [revealed preference theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revealed_preference) applies the idea that you can understand consumer preferences by observing their actual (purchasing) behaviour.
Loosely adapted to software development methodology, we can make the analogy that despite what software development teams might tell you, looking at how they actually work reveals that waterfall thinking persists in the workplace.

Revealed preference theory’s weaknesses include its inability to reveal which options people discarded before making a choice.
A software development team may show off the classic agile methodology books on their bookshelf, while the forces and constraints on how they actually work remain hidden.

## Agile manifesto obsolescence

Today, the agile manifesto has reached obsolescence.
It no longer sounds reasonable to base current practice on something a group of middle-aged white men wrote 25 years ago.
At the same time, the agile manifest largely achieved its goals by mostly becoming the dominant way of thinking about software development.

Even if teams don’t appear to follow those principles and practices in daily work, they don’t argue for the waterfall-shaped opposites either.
This seems to leave a muddy middle ground, that we no longer translate into new and opinionated practices.

## Modern software development practices

Books on software development practices such as [Extreme Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_programming), [Scrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrum_(project_management)), [Lean Software Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_software_development) and [Kanban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanban_(development)) all appeared around the five-year period from 1999 to 2004.
For example, Alistair Cockburn’s 2002 book Agile Software Development Ecosystems describes most of the methods we have focused on since then:

1. Scrum
2. Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM)
3. Crystal Methods
4. Feature-Driven Development (FDD)
5. Lean Development (LD)
6. Extreme Programming (XP)
7. Adaptive Software Development (ASD)

Kanban doesn’t appear in the book’s table of contents, because it only became popular in the five years that followed.

## The last practice

Looking back, it looks like some kind of development methodology golden age produced the greatest hits of the genre in the years 2000–2010.
Since then we’ve only had progressively worse remixes.
With luck, your team at least has a regular retrospective – the original agile development practice.

Ultimately, software development methods live on, even if only as pale shadows of Scrum, Kanban and Lean software development, while software development _methodology_ went out of fashion.
We continue to design and build software, but don’t pay much attention to designing and building software development _processes_.
